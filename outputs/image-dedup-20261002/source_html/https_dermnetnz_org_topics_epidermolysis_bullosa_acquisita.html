What is epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita (EBA) is a rare, acquired, autoimmune, mucocutaneous blistering disease that occurs at sites of minor trauma.

Unlike epidermolysis bullosa, EBA is not inherited and usually presents in adulthood.

Who gets epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

EBA is considered one of the rarest subepidermal blistering diseases. The exact incidence and prevalence remain unknown, though the annual incidence is estimated to be less than 0.5 cases per million individuals. While it typically affects adults (50-70 years old), paediatric cases have been reported, including a neonatal case caused by transfer of antibodies across the placenta.

Gender, racial, ethnic, or geographical predispositions have not been conclusively established. However, some studies report a greater prevalence amongst certain African-American populations.

Patients with EBA may have concomitant systemic disorders such as:

  • Rheumatoid arthritis
  • Systemic lupus erythematosus
  • Diabetes mellitus
  • Inflammatory bowel disease, particularly Crohn disease
  • Amyloidosis
  • Lymphoma and multiple myeloma.

The relationship between EBA and these disorders remains unclear.

What causes epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

While the exact aetiology remains unknown, epidermolysis bullosa acquisita is considered an autoimmune disorder characterised by tissue-bound autoantibodies (IgG) against the NC1 domain of type VII collagen.

Type VII collagen is the major structural component of anchoring fibrils, which attach the basement membrane to the underlying papillary dermis at the dermo-epidermal junction. Loss of fibrils due to autoimmune inflammation and destruction results in subepidermal blistering within the lamina densa.

Potential triggering factors reported in the literature include:

  • Ultraviolet radiation
  • Antibiotics (specifically penicillin, gentamycin, and vancomycin)
  • Contact allergy to metals.

Genetics may also play a role in the development of EBA, as human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class II genotypes are more common amongst those with EBA.

What are the clinical features of epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita can be categorised into two common presentations: classical (mechanobullous) EBA and inflammatory (non-mechanobullous) EBA.

A mixture of inflammatory and non-inflammatory features may co-exist.

Classical EBA

  • Characterised by skin fragility with tense bullae, vesicles, and erosions on non-inflamed skin.
  • Lesions are localised to sites prone to friction and minor trauma eg, the extensor surfaces of the hands, feet, toes, sacrum, elbows, and knees.
  • Lesions heal with scarring, atrophy, milia, and dyspigmentation.
  • Mucosal involvement may occur, but is not predominant.
  • Severe forms may resemble hereditary dystrophic epidermolysis bullosa and feature scarring alopecia, digit deformity, nail dystrophy and loss, and oesophageal stenosis and webbing.

Inflammatory EBA

Inflammatory EBA variants

Clinical features

Bullous pemphigoid-like variant
  • Widespread, inflammatory blistering eruption over the trunk, skin folds, and extremities
  • Presents with tense blisters and erosions on inflamed or urticarial skin
  • Large areas of inflamed or urticarial skin may be seen without blistering
  • Pruritus is common
  • Skin fragility, scarring, and milia formation are not prominent
  • Mucous membranes are not commonly involved

Mucous membrane pemphigoid-like variant
  • Predominantly affects mucous membranes
  • Causes erosions and scarring on the mucosal surfaces of the mouth, conjunctiva, upper oesophagus, nasopharynx, genitals, and anus
  • May result in dysphagia, weight loss, malnutrition, and asphyxiation due to oesophageal stenosis and airway scarring

Linear IgA disease-like variant
  • Tense bullae and vesicles are arranged in an annular or clustered configuration
  • Linear IgA deposits are seen in the basement membrane zone
  • Tends to produce few scars or milium cysts
  • Mucosal involvement is common

Brunsting-Perry pemphigoid-like variant
  • Rare form that is localised to the head and neck
  • Characterised by blisters, erosions, haemorrhagic crusts, residual scarring, and alopecia
  • Little to no mucosal involvement

What are the complications of epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

Complications of cutaneous lesions:

  • Secondary skin infections, typically Staphylococcus and Streptococcus
  • Atrophic scarring
  • Milia formation
  • Nail dystrophy or anonychia
  • Impaired movement or mobility secondary to joint contractures
  • Anxiety and low mood.

Complications of mucosal lesions:

  • Conjunctival blistering
  • Symblephara formation
  • Ankyloglossia
  • Nasal synechiae
  • Trichiasis
  • Oesophageal erosions and strictures
  • Laryngeal scarring and strictures
  • Dysphagia and weight loss.

How is epidermolysis bullosa acquisita diagnosed?

Diagnosis of epidermolysis bullosa acquisita is based on a combination of clinical and immunohistological findings:

  • Clinically consistent features
  • Absence of a family history of bullous disorders
  • Histology demonstrating a subepidermal blister
  • Direct immunofluorescence showing linear deposition of IgG along the basement membrane zone (BMZ), also known as the dermo-epidermal junction.
  • Autoantibodies against type VII collagen (COLVII) or surrogate evidence of COLVII as the target antigen.

An initial evaluation should include:

  • Lesional skin biopsy
    • Histology reveals a subepidermal blister with variable infiltration of mixed inflammatory cells in the dermis.
    • The extent of inflammatory infiltrate usually correlates with the level of inflammation clinically observed.
    • Milia cysts and scarring can be seen in older lesions.
  • Perilesional skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence (DIF)
    • DIF reveals deposition of immunoglobulin (IgG more often than IgM or IgA) and C3 linearly along the BMZ — this is sensitive but not specific for EBA.
    • Blood vessels are uninvolved.
  • Indirect immunofluorescence (IIF) of serum
    • Circulating anti-BMZ antibodies can be detected in 50% of patients with EBA.
    • These are primarily IgG-class antibodies, but IgA has been reported.

Further investigations to confirm the diagnosis include:

  • Salt-split skin testing (SST)
    • DIF shows immune deposition on the dermal side (blister floor) of the patient’s skin
    • IIF detects circulating antibodies which bind to the dermal side of the skin substrate
    • Deposition on the epidermal side (blister roof) would instead indicate bullous pemphigoid
  • Serration pattern analysis on DIF — reveals a u-serrated pattern along the BMZ
  • Detection of antibodies against type VII collagen
    • Western immunoblotting
    • Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)
    • Immunoprecipitation
  • Immunoelectron microscopy (IEM)
    • Demonstrates antibody deposition within the sublamina densa
    • Considered the gold standard for diagnosis
    • Use is primarily limited to research settings
  • Transmission electron microscopy — can identify loss of anchoring fibrils within the BMZ
  • Fluorescence overlay antigen mapping (FOAM) — identifies the level of antibody deposition using reference markers of known BMZ components.

What is the differential diagnosis for epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

  • Bullous pemphigoid
  • Brunsting-Perry cicatricial pemphigoid
  • Linear IgA bullous disease (LABD)
  • Bullous systemic lupus erythematosus
  • Mucous membrane pemphigoid (MMP)
  • Dermatitis herpetiformis
  • Pemphigus vulgaris
  • Pemphigus foliaceus
  • Porphyria cutanea tarda (particularly with mechanobullous EBA)
  • Pseudoporphyria

What is the treatment for epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

The goal of treatment for epidermolysis bullosa acquisita is to prevent disease progression and complications and to promote healing. Treatment should be tailored to the individual.

General measures

Patients should be advised to:

  • Avoid skin trauma and excessive rubbing
  • Avoid overusing hot water and harsh soaps or bodywashes
  • Employ sun protection methods eg, avoid prolonged sun exposure, use sunscreens, wear UV-protective clothing
  • Avoid using adhesive bandages and tape on the skin
  • Wear loose clothing and comfortable shoes
  • Avoid hard, brittle, or highly acidic food if there is mucosal involvement
  • Maintain skin hygiene and look out for signs of infection.

Multidisciplinary management (eg, gastroenterology, dentistry, and ophthalmology) is usually required where there is mucosal involvement.

Specific measures

Several treatment options are available, though there is no consensus on the most effective treatment due to the rarity of EBA.

Interventions reported in literature include:

  • Systemic corticosteroids 0.5-2 mg/kg/day
  • Corticosteroid-sparing immunosuppressive or immunomodulating agents
    • Methotrexate
    • Azathioprine
    • Ciclosporin
    • Mycophenolate mofetil
    • Colchicine
    • Dapsone
    • Rituximab
    • Daclizumab (monoclonal antibody against Tac antigen or CD25)
  • Intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) therapy
  • Plasmapheresis and immunoadsorption
  • Extracorporeal photopheresis
  • Tetracyclines eg, doxycycline and minocycline – evidence for their efficacy remains unclear.

What is the outcome for epidermolysis bullosa acquisita?

Epidermolysis bullosa acquisita is a chronic, relapsing-remitting disease and those affected have a normal life expectancy. Prognostic factors are not fully understood, but prognosis is considered more favourable in children compared to adults.

Treatment aims to control the disease and often requires long-term maintenance therapy, which also poses health risks.
